About Work Press and writing Roots

My grandfather's family crossed into India during Partition and started over in Delhi with very little. He rarely spoke about that. What he spoke about were other people: Guru Nanak, Bhagat Singh, Swami Vivekananda, people who gave far more than they ever took. I heard those stories so often as a child that I assumed every family told them.

My mother never told those stories. She simply lived them, and I learnt more from watching her than from anything I was taught. Between the two of them, seva stopped being a word and became a habit of the house: you look after people who are not yours to look after.

Over the years, that habit has settled into one belief I try to live by.

A society that hopes to be just, prosperous and humane must imbibe a mother's ability to love selflessly, a teacher's ability to nurture unselfishly and a soldier's ability to sacrifice unflinchingly.

Where it began

University was where that belief first met the real world. As Vice-President and then President of the student council, I found myself raising students' issues with the administration, negotiating between groups who rarely agreed, and learning how political and social realities are far messier than any textbook. Through the legal aid cell, I spent weekends in slum clusters helping people understand, and claim, rights the law already gave them.

One moment from those years has never left me. After the Nirbhaya case shook the country, a group of us at the university decided we could not simply watch. We wrote and directed a street play on juvenile justice and performed it for judges of the Delhi High Court. Several of the changes we argued for on that stage later found their way into the law Parliament passed in the aftermath. Just as meaningful were the students who came up to us afterwards, saying the play had helped them see how complicated the law and the system really are, and who then joined the protests themselves. That was when I understood that people change systems once they understand them.

Learning from the road

After building and then losing my first organisation, Khevaiya, I did not want to theorise about India from a distance. So I travelled, across more than 100 districts in 14 states, sitting in public schools, hospitals, district offices and people's homes. I met uncertainty, poverty and hunger. I also met more hope, diversity and love than I knew existed.

In Havipura, a small village deep in the Chambal valley, a man I had never met took me to his home. He had almost nothing in it, yet he fed me and looked after me with a warmth I had rarely seen. Why teachers

The road, two years in district government and the classrooms of Patiala have all taught me one thing: teachers are the levers of any larger social change. A society that gives teachers their rightful place, in public life and in its priorities, is a society headed towards prosperity, equality and justice.

I once met a teacher in a small village school with barely any working infrastructure. He was still teaching every day, through stories and activities, with whatever he had. When I asked him why, he said:

His own child studied in a government senior secondary school. He was not asking anyone to believe in a system he had given up on; he had placed his own future inside it. In him I saw all three figures I grew up with: a mother's love for children who were not his own, a teacher's patience with whatever he had, and a soldier's quiet refusal to leave his post. Change begins from within, and it begins with people like him.

What I'm building now

That is why I am now building an organisation of my own in education. Its purpose is simple: to stand behind teachers like him, so that every child's right to learn does not depend on what their parents can afford.

Fixing education in India is not a problem anyone solves in a single career. It is slow, stubborn and often thankless, and there will be failures along the way. I have made peace with that. Some problems are worth giving a whole life to, and public service through better schools, fairer access and real learning in every classroom is mine. It is the cause on whose altar I would gladly give my life.

Capt. Manoj Kumar Pandey said it better than I ever could:

Work

Every role I have taken has been a different door into the same room: making the systems we all depend on work for the people at the very end of the line.

Program Lead, STEM Collective · Sanjhi Sikhiya, Patiala · 2025 – 2026

Opening the world of science to girls in Punjab's government schools

The STEM Collective began with a simple question: what would it take for a girl in a government school in Patiala to picture herself as a scientist or an engineer? I carried that question all the way to the classroom.

It started with a landscape study and grew into a four-year programme for the district's government senior secondary schools, built hand in hand with the district administration, school leaders and education officials. Along the way, more than eight partners, including Thapar University, Udayan, SAMA and PI-RAHI, came to one table. Donors gave about ₹40 lakh worth of devices. And more than 300 people gathered at a first-of-its-kind STEM conclave to ask how Punjab's government schools could lead, not follow, in science.

Young Leaders Program · Jan Suraj, Bihar · 2023

Learning how a people's movement is built, one village at a time

In Bihar I went back to the ground to learn something no office could teach: how ordinary citizens are brought together around a shared idea of change. District Development Fellow · Government of Punjab, Fazilka · 2020 – 2022

Two years on Punjab's border, making government work where it is needed most

Fazilka sits on India's border with Pakistan, far from the places where policy is usually made. For two years I worked inside its district administration, and the work touched almost every part of public life.

We built modern public libraries with room for more than 200 readers. Under Project AABAD, more than 20 departments came together for 30 border villages that had long been left behind. Wasteland in 75 villages became dense Miyawaki forests. Abohar, once ranked 380th in India for cleanliness, climbed to 105th in a single year, and became the first city in the country to tax and ban plastic at the municipal level, alongside a dedicated welfare fund for its sanitation workers.

When the pandemic came, I helped coordinate the district's response and rethink how vaccines reached more than 200 villages. When elections came, we made booths across 40 border villages accessible, and every voter with special needs turned out to vote. Four of these efforts were recognised by the Chief Minister of Punjab.

Co-founder · Khevaiya Innovation and Research Foundation, New Delhi · 2017 – 2020

Betting everything on the classroom

Straight out of law school, with no funding and no safety net, my co-founders and I set out to answer a question most people had stopped asking: why do good education policies so rarely change what happens inside a classroom?

We spent months listening to teachers, principals and officials, then built a low-cost, school-wide model to close that gap. Where it ran, children were more engaged, and more of them passed. We reached more than 200 schools across Delhi-NCR, trained over 200 teachers and school leaders, and made it to the final round of two national incubators out of more than 250 startups. Khevaiya did not survive, but everything I have done since has been built on what it taught me.

Where I learnt to lead

Law school gave me the language of rights; student politics taught me how hard it is to make those rights real. My fellow students elected me Vice-President and then President of the university's student council, and I spent those years carrying their concerns, from hostel security to scholarships, to the university administration on behalf of more than 10,000 students.

We also built things together: festivals and national and international conferences that brought in more than 120 institutions and a lakh students. Every one of them taught me how to hold very different people to one shared purpose.

Legal Aid Cell and court internships · Delhi · 2012 – 2017

Taking the law to the people who needed it most

For four years, weekends meant the slum clusters of Dwarka, where our legal aid cell helped families understand rights the law already promised them, and gave more than 50 people free legal help. With the Delhi State Legal Services Authority, I trained over 300 para-legal volunteers to carry that work into their own communities, and in the Delhi High Court and district courts I saw how far justice can sit from the people it is meant for.

Founding member · Lok Jagrukta Society, New Delhi · 2015 – 2017

Using the Right to Information to win back dignity

A group of us students started a small non-profit with one conviction: that ordinary citizens can make the system answer. Through RTI applications and research done alongside residents, we pushed until more than 600 households in a Dwarka slum had working community toilets. It was my first lesson that dignity is often won through patience and paperwork.
